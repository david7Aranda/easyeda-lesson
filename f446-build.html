The Wiring Tools you'll use

Wiring Tools—

Drawing Tools lines are not wires. They look the same on screen but carry no connection to the PCB.

ToolKeyUse it for
WireWShort connections between nearby pins. Clicking a pin also starts a wire.
Net LabelNNaming a connection, so it can join pins anywhere without a long wire.
VCC / GND flagsCtrl+Q Ctrl+GPower and ground. Rename the VCC flag to +3V3 in its properties.
No Connect FlagMarking a pin you're leaving unused on purpose.
Four tools cover nearly the whole MCU schematic. Bus and bus entry are for very large designs; we won't need them. The VCC flag defaults to the name VCC. We'll rename it +3V3 so the name says what it is, then copy that flag for every 3.3 V pin.

Same name, same connection

  • Every Net Label, Net Flag or Net Port with the same name is one connection, even with no wire between them.
  • This keeps a big schematic readable: a short wire and a label at each pin.
  • Names must match exactly. +3V3 and 3V3 are two different nets.
  • Put a No Connect Flag on any pin you leave empty, so the Design manager knows it's on purpose.
Click once to show that the three +3V3 flags are one net. Click again for the No Connect flag. This is the technique we'll use for every block of the F446: a short stub on the pin, then a net label.

Fewer pins to wire and route, and everything we learn carries straight over to Board A.

Both boards use the same STM32F4 family, so the core circuit is nearly identical. We start with the 64-pin F446 because there's less to wire and route, which makes it a better first board. The full part-selection talk, mcu-parts.html, goes deeper into choosing between them.

What carries over to Board A

Sameon both boards

  • Power and decoupling: 100 nF per VDD pin plus a bulk capacitor; VDDA filtered.
  • Reset and boot: 100 nF on NRST, 10 kΩ from BOOT0 to GND.
  • Crystals: the same start-up check and load-capacitor math.
  • Debug: the same SWD header on PA13 and PA14.
  • USB: the same PA11 and PA12 pins.

Changeson Board A

  • 100 pins: more VDD and VSS pairs, so more 100 nF capacitors.
  • Two VCAP pins: 2.2 µF on each, instead of one 4.7 µF.
  • Ethernet: an external PHY chip and an RJ45 jack with magnetics.
  • Clock: at 168 MHz, USB's 48 MHz comes straight from the main PLL.
  • Pin numbers: every pin moves, so use the F407's own symbol.
This is why the F446 is a good first build: about three quarters of the work transfers to Board A unchanged. The VCAP difference is the one to remember; it's a common mistake when people copy a schematic between packages.

Seven blocks, built in this order

Every STM32 board needs blocks 1 to 5 just to start and be programmed. Block 6 gives it power and USB from one connector, and block 7 proves it's alive. The number on each block is its step and the slide where we wire it.

Meet your symbol

64 pins: 14 for power and control, 50 general I/O. The symbol puts 32 on each side for easy wiring; the real chip has 16 on each of its four sides.

    Power and groundVDD ×4, VDDA, VBAT, VSS ×4, VSSA
  • 3

  • 4
    Crystal pinsPH0, PH1 for the 8 MHz HSE; PC14, PC15 for the 32.768 kHz LSE
  • 4

  • 42

This is the exact symbol from our schematic, pin numbers and all. Click through the groups. The first two groups aren't I/O at all, which is why a 64-pin chip has 50 GPIOs. The crystal, debug and USB pins are ordinary GPIOs that we reserve. That leaves 42 for the actual project.

1Power and ground

  • Pins 19, 32, 48, 64 (VDD) and 1 (VBAT): a short stub and a +3V3 flag on each.
  • Pins 18, 31, 47, 63 (VSS): a GND flag on each.
  • One 100 nF capacitor per power pin, and one 4.7 µF for the whole chip.
VBAT powers the real-time clock's backup domain. With no coin cell, tie it to 3.3 V with its own 100 nF, as the datasheet says. On the schematic the capacitors can sit together; on the PCB each 100 nF must be right next to its own pin.

2Analog supply and VCAP

  • Pin 13 (VDDA) gets VDDA: 3.3 V through a ferrite bead, with 1 µF + 100 nF. Pin 12 (VSSA) to GND.
  • Pin 30 (VCAP_1): one 4.7 µF low-ESR ceramic to GND. Nothing else.

Never connect VCAP to 3.3 V. It's the output of the chip's internal 1.2 V core regulator.

The ADC measures against VDDA, so noise on VDDA becomes noise in every reading; the ferrite bead and capacitors filter it. The bead is optional for a first board: a 0 Ω resistor in its place works. VCAP is the big one: wrong value, high-ESR capacitor, or tied to 3.3 V, and the chip won't run or won't show up in the debugger.

3Reset and boot

  • Pin 7 (NRST) gets NRST: 100 nF to GND. A reset button to GND is optional. The chip has its own pull-up.
  • Pin 60 (BOOT0) gets BOOT0: 10 kΩ to GND, so the chip runs your program from flash.
  • Want ST's built-in bootloader later? Add a jumper that pulls BOOT0 to 3.3 V.
Floating BOOT0 is a classic first-board bug: the chip sometimes boots into ST's bootloader instead of your program, and it looks like the board is dead. The NRST net also goes to the debug header in step 5, so the programmer can reset the chip.

4Crystals

  • Pins 5, 6 (PH0, PH1): the 8 MHz HSE crystal and two load capacitors.
  • Pins 3, 4 (PC14, PC15): the 32.768 kHz LSE crystal, only if you use the real-time clock.
  • Each load capacitor is 2 × (CL − Cstray). Check the crystal with the calculator in the parts talk.
The 8 MHz crystal feeds the PLL. At 180 MHz, USB's 48 MHz comes from the second PLL, PLLSAI. The crystal calculator is slide 17 of mcu-parts.html. Pick a low-ESR 32.768 kHz crystal with about 6 pF load capacitance; the common 12.5 pF watch crystals fail the start-up check on this chip.

5Debug header

  • Pin 46 (PA13) is SWDIO, pin 49 (PA14) is SWCLK.
  • A 5-pin header: +3V3, SWDIO, SWCLK, NRST, GND. Print the pin names on the silkscreen.
  • Optional: pin 55 (PB3) as SWO, for printing debug messages over the same cable.

Any ST-LINK programmer works, including the one built into a Nucleo board.

This is how the chip gets programmed and debugged, so it is not optional. Never reuse PA13 and PA14 for anything else in firmware, or you lock yourself out until you connect under reset. The header is our own simple pinout; any order works as long as the silkscreen says what each pin is.

6USB-C power and data

  • VBUS (5 V) goes to a 3.3 V regulator that makes +3V3. Use the capacitors its datasheet asks for.
  • CC1 and CC2 each get their own 5.1 kΩ to GND, or USB-C chargers won't turn on.
  • D− to pin 44 (PA11), D+ to pin 45 (PA12), through an ESD protection chip.
  • The connector has two D+ and two D− pins. Join each pair.
One shared resistor across both CC pins is the Raspberry Pi 4 mistake: some chargers then refuse to give 5 V. The F446 has the USB pull-up built in, so D+ and D− connect straight to the pins. USB also needs the crystal, because the internal RC oscillator isn't accurate enough.

7Signs of life

  • An LED with a 1 kΩ resistor on pin 21 (PA5).
  • A button to GND on pin 2 (PC13). Turn on the internal pull-up in firmware.
  • A 3-pin UART header on pins 16, 17 (PA2 TX, PA3 RX) for a USB-serial adapter.
  • These are the same pins the Nucleo-F446RE uses, so its example code runs unchanged.
The first firmware for any new board is a blinking LED, then a hello message over the UART. Matching the Nucleo's pins means people can practice on a Nucleo while the boards are being made, then load the same code on ours.

On the PCB: what goes where

  • Each 100 nF right at its VDD and VSS pin pair. The 4.7 µF on VCAP right at pin 30.
  • The crystal and its capacitors tight against pins 5 and 6, with nothing routed underneath.
  • USB-C and the SWD header on the board edge, on the side nearest their pins.
  • The real chip numbers pins counter-clockwise from the dot: 1–16 down the left side.
This is where the symbol and the real chip differ: the symbol has 32 pins a side, the package has 16 a side. Look at where the key pins actually sit. The power pairs are at the four corners, the crystal pins on the upper left, USB and SWD on the upper right. That decides where the connectors go.

Questions people usually ask

Do we really need the 8 MHz crystal?The internal 16 MHz oscillator is about 1% accurate. Fine for blinking and UART, not for USB.
Why 64 pins but only 50 I/O?14 pins are power, ground, VCAP, NRST and BOOT0.
Why is BOOT0 pulled to GND?So the chip runs your program from flash at power-up. Pulled high, it starts ST's built-in bootloader instead.
What happens if VCAP goes to 3.3 V?It pushes 3.3 V into the 1.2 V core regulator's output. The chip won't run and can be damaged.
What do we program it with?An ST-LINK on the SWD header. The one built into a Nucleo board works too.
Why does the Design manager show errors?Usually an unused pin with no No Connect Flag, or two net names that almost match.
Can we skip USB?Yes. Feed 3.3 V into a header and program over SWD. You lose the 5 V input and USB data.
Where do the motor and sensor pins go?Decide in CubeMX first, then label those pins on the schematic.
Leave this up while people start working. If a question comes up that isn't here, write it down; it probably belongs on this slide next time.
