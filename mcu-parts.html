Picking parts for a custom STM32 board

Two boards, one method, and how to choose the crystal that keeps them on time.

Use the arrow keys or a clicker to move through. Press N for speaker notes.

Today is about the decisions that happen before anyone opens a PCB editor: which microcontroller, which package, which support parts, and which crystal. We'll use two real boards as running examples. Board A is a robot I/O hub on an STM32F407VGT6. Board B is a fast control-loop board on an STM32F446RET6.

One method for every board

Steps 1 to 5 and 7 to 8 are about parts. Step 6, the crystal, gets its own section because it is where most first boards fail to start.

This is the order to make decisions in. The mistake people make is starting at step 3, picking a chip they like, and discovering at step 4 that the pins don't work out. Requirements and the pin count come first.

Count the pins before you pick the package

Add up every pin each interface uses. Board A needs 79 of the F407's 82 GPIOs, which is why it needs the 100-pin package; a 64-pin part would run out where the dashed line is. Board B fits in 44 of 50. These budgets are examples; edit the PINS list at the top of the file to match your design. Leave some spare pins: boards always grow.

Read the part number

Every letter in an STM32 part number means something. The two you most often get wrong are the pin-count letter and the flash letter. The TR suffix just means the chips ship on a tape-and-reel for pick-and-place machines; that's why the library search shows STM32F446RET6 and STM32F446RET6TR as separate rows.

Lock the pinout in CubeMX

  • STM32CubeMX is ST's free configuration tool. Pick the exact part, then turn on each peripheral you need.
  • It assigns pins and flags conflicts when two peripherals want the same one.
  • Its clock tab checks your crystal and PLL settings. We'll use it in the crystal section.
  • Export the pin list and freeze it before PCB layout starts.
Each pin can only do one job at a time. Ethernet on the F407 and Quad-SPI on the F446 both use fixed groups of pins, so turn those on first and fit everything else around them. The colors on the chip are illustrative, not the real F446 pin map; do this exercise in CubeMX with your actual list.

Power: LDO or buck?

  • Add up the current: MCU, Ethernet PHY, sensors, LEDs. Then add margin.
  • A linear regulator (LDO) turns the extra voltage into heat.
Pheat = (Vin − 3.3 V) × I
  • From a 5 V USB supply an LDO is fine. From a 12 V robot battery, use a buck converter.

This slide is just arithmetic, which makes it a good one to work through with the room. For Board A on a 12 V robot battery, the LDO option cooks itself. A common pattern is a buck converter down to 3.3 V, or a buck to 5 V followed by a small LDO for a quiet analog rail. Remember the Ethernet PHY draws real current too.

Parts that connect the MCU to the world

Board ARobot I/O hub

  • Ethernet PHY (RMII)The F407 has the MAC; the PHY does the analog work. Check where its 25 or 50 MHz clock comes from.
  • RJ45 jack with built-in magneticsSaves a separate transformer and a lot of board space.
  • 2 × CAN transceiversChoose 3.3 V parts so they connect straight to the MCU.
  • USB connector + ESD protectionAnything a person plugs in needs ESD protection.
  • Locking sensor connectorsRobots vibrate. Use keyed, latching connectors.

Board BFast control loop

  • Quad-SPI NOR flash3.3 V, quad I/O. Size it for your logs or lookup tables.
  • Audio codec or I²S DACConnects to the SAI. Check the sample rates you need.
  • Gate driver + MOSFETsInputs must accept 3.3 V logic from the timer outputs.
  • Current-sense amplifierFeeds the ADC for motor control. Match its output range to the ADC's 0 to 3.3 V.
  • USB-C connector + ESD protectionSame rule as Board A.

Many STM32F4 pins tolerate 5 V, but not when they're used as analog inputs. Check for "FT" in the datasheet pin table before wiring a 5 V sensor.

This is where your parts list grows fastest. For each interface part, check three things: it runs at 3.3 V logic, it's in stock, and there's a reference design to copy. For the Ethernet PHY, the clock question links straight into the crystal section.

Check stock and assembly

  • Confirm the part is in stock and active, not "not recommended for new designs".
  • At JLCPCB, Extended parts add a loading fee per part type (about $3 each when this was written). Use Basic parts for passives where you can.
  • Check the footprint against the datasheet's package drawing.
  • Pick packages your team can assemble or rework. LQFP pins are visible and solderable by hand; QFN and BGA are much harder.

Why add a crystal at all?

  • The STM32's internal 16 MHz RC oscillator (HSI) is about 1% accurate. Fine for LEDs and most UART links.
  • USB, Ethernet and a real-time clock need far tighter timing than that.
  • HSE: a 4 to 26 MHz crystal that feeds the PLL and system clock.
  • LSE: a 32.768 kHz crystal that keeps the RTC on time.

Clock error in parts per million (log scale). Shorter is tighter.

One percent sounds small until you convert it: that's 10,000 parts per million. USB full speed allows 0.25%, which is 2,500 ppm. An Ethernet PHY wants its clock within 100 ppm. And if you want an RTC that drifts less than a minute a month, you need about 23 ppm. A typical crystal sits at 10 to 30 ppm, which is why every one of these needs a crystal.

Pick a frequency that divides cleanly

The PLL turns the crystal into the core clock and USB's exact 48 MHz. 8 MHz and 25 MHz are the usual picks. ST recommends dividing down to 2 MHz first to keep PLL jitter low.

Board A works out neatly: 8 MHz divided by 4 is 2 MHz, times 168 is 336 MHz, divided by 2 gives 168 MHz for the core and divided by 7 gives exactly 48 MHz for USB. Board B at 180 MHz is the interesting one: the main PLL gives 360 MHz, and 360 divided by 7 is 51.4 MHz, which USB won't accept. The F446 has a second PLL, PLLSAI, that can make the 48 MHz on its own. The other option is to run at 168 MHz. Also: 180 MHz requires the regulator's over-drive mode. CubeMX's clock tab does all of this math for you.

  • ESRThe loss the oscillator has to overcome. Lower starts more easily.
  • Shunt capacitance (C0)The crystal's own capacitance. Goes into the start-up check.
  • Tolerance and stabilityError at 25 °C, and drift over temperature, in ppm.

Will it start?

gmcrit = 4 × ESR × (2πF)² × (C0 + CL)²The oscillator must be at least 5 times stronger than this. So gmcrit must stay under the MCU's limit:

Example: the 8 MHz crystal

ESR 80 Ω, F 8 MHz, C0 3 pF, CL 20 pF
4 × 80 × (2π × 8×10⁶)² × (23×10⁻¹²)² = 0.43 mA/V
0.43 mA/V is under the 1 mA/V limit. Gain margin is about 12, so it starts reliably.
This formula is from ST's oscillator design guide, AN2867. gm critical is how much gain the crystal needs; the MCU's oscillator has a minimum gain, and we want at least five times what's needed. The limits are that minimum divided by five. The LSE limit is tiny, which is why 32.768 kHz crystals cause so much trouble; the calculator two slides on shows a real example.

Size the load capacitors

CL = (C1 × C2) ÷ (C1 + C2) + Cstray
  • With equal capacitors: C1 = C2 = 2 × (CL − Cstray)
  • Cstray is the pins plus the traces: a few pF. ST's own example uses 5 pF.
  • Use C0G (NP0) capacitors. Their value doesn't drift with temperature or voltage.
  • Common mistake: 20 pF capacitors on a 20 pF crystal. The crystal then sees only about 15 pF and runs slightly fast.
The two capacitors are in series as far as the crystal is concerned, which is why you double the number. Stray capacitance is a guess on a new board; a few picofarads is typical, and ST uses 5 pF in its example. If the measured frequency is off, adjust these capacitors on the next revision.

Lay out the crystal carefully

  • Put the crystal and both capacitors right next to OSC_IN and OSC_OUT.
  • Keep both traces short, about equal, and free of vias.
  • Ground the capacitors together, close to the MCU's VSS pin.
  • Surround the oscillator with a ground guard ring. Route nothing underneath it.
  • Keep motor drivers, switching regulators and fast buses well away.
The oscillator is a tiny-signal, high-impedance circuit, so it picks up noise easily and stray capacitance shifts its frequency. On Board B, keep the crystal on the opposite side of the board from the motor's power stage. These guidelines follow the layout section of ST's AN2867.

For Board B next to a motor power stage, an active oscillator is worth discussing: it's much less sensitive to noise and needs no gain-margin check. For Board A, one clock could serve both the MCU and the Ethernet PHY. A passive crystal is still the default for most boards because it's cheap and simple.

Questions to work through together

Board A

  1. Does the Ethernet PHY get its own crystal, or a clock from the MCU?
  2. 79 of 82 pins is tight. What would we give up to keep spares?
  3. Will any 5 V sensor ever reach an analog pin?

Board B

  1. 180 MHz with PLLSAI for USB, or 168 MHz and a simpler clock tree?
  2. Crystal or active oscillator next to the motor's power stage?
  3. How much Quad-SPI flash do the logs really need?

Both

  1. Which Extended parts could become Basic parts?
  2. Do we need the RTC, and so the 32.768 kHz crystal, at all?
  3. Who assembles the board: JLCPCB's machines or us by hand?
Pick two or three of these depending on time. There's no single right answer to any of them; the point is to make the trade-off on purpose.
