Designing a circuit board in EasyEDA Pro

From a schematic to a board you can order, all in one browser tab.

Use the arrow keys or a clicker to move through the lesson. Press N for speaker notes.

Welcome. Today we design a real circuit board from start to finish: a push button that lights an LED. By the end, everyone will have a design that could be sent to a factory. Have students open pro.easyeda.com/editor in a browser tab now so it's signed in and ready.

What EasyEDA Pro is

  • A free circuit board design tool that runs in your browser at pro.easyeda.com/editor. There's a desktop app too.
  • Projects save to your account, so you can pick up on any computer.
  • A built-in parts library from LCSC. Most parts already have a symbol, a footprint and a 3D model.
  • Connected to JLCPCB, so you can order boards straight from the Order menu.
  • EasyEDA Standard is the older editor. Tutorials that use it will look different from what you see today.
EasyEDA Pro is free and runs in a browser, which is why we use it in class. The big advantage is the parts library: when you search for a part, it usually comes with its symbol, footprint and 3D model already done. Warn students that many YouTube tutorials use EasyEDA Standard, the older editor, so menus won't match.

2Place the parts

  • Shift+F opens the place-device dialog. You can also press S and use the Library panel.
  • Search by value and package, like 330 0603, or paste an LCSC part number.
  • Check the preview: the part needs a symbol and a footprint.
  • Click to place. Space rotates. Esc stops placing.
  • If JLCPCB will assemble your board, "Basic" parts avoid an extra setup fee.

Our board needs four parts: a 2-pin connector J1 for 5 volts in, a push button SW1, a 330 ohm resistor R1, and a red LED D1. Search by value plus package size; 0603 is a small surface-mount size. Have students place all four now, roughly where the slide shows them. The part numbers and results in the slide are illustrations; their search results will differ.

3Wire the schematic

  • W starts a wire. Click a pin to start, click to add a corner, click a pin to finish.
  • Right-click or Esc ends the wire. Hold Shift to keep it straight.
  • N places a net label. Two wires with the same net name are connected, even with nothing drawn between them.
  • Ground and power symbols work the same way: every GND symbol is one net.

Click once to show the wires drawing in order: connector to button, button to resistor, resistor to LED. Click again for ground: notice there's no wire between the two GND symbols, but they're connected because they share a name. Last click shows H, which highlights everything on one net. That's the fastest way to check a connection.

4Check the schematic

  • Design›Check DRC. Problems appear in the bottom panel; click one to jump to it.
  • Click each part and confirm its Footprint field in the properties panel.
  • Every designator must be unique: R1, R2, D1…
  • H highlights a whole net, handy for tracing a connection.

Step one: the check finds a problem. The wire to the LED stops just short of the pin. It looks connected on screen but isn't. Click again to fix it and re-run. This is the single most common schematic mistake, so make students run the check every time.

5Convert to a PCB

  • Design›Update/Convert Schematic to PCB
  • Review the list of parts and nets, then click Apply Changes.
  • Footprints land on the PCB. The thin lines, called flylines or ratsnest, show every connection you still have to route.
  • Changed the schematic later? Design›Import Changes from Schematic brings the edits across.

This is the hand-off between the two editors. The dialog lists everything that will be added. After applying, parts arrive in a jumbled pile. That's normal. The flylines are the important part: each one is a connection from the schematic that still needs a copper track.

6Outline and design rules

  • Place›Board Outline, then pick a rectangle and drag out the board.
  • Design›Design Rule sets track widths and spacing.

The magenta line is the board edge: it's literally where the factory cuts. Then set design rules before routing so the editor can warn you as you draw. The values here are conservative starting points, not hard limits. Ask: why might a power track need to be wider than a signal track? Answer: it carries more current.

6Lay out the parts

  • Drag parts inside the outline. Space rotates.
  • Put connectors and buttons on the edge, where hands and cables can reach.
  • Keep parts that connect close together. Watch the flylines get shorter and stop crossing.
  • Point polarized parts like LEDs the same way.
  • Add mounting holes now, before routing boxes you in.
Layout is most of the skill in PCB design. Click to watch the parts move into place: as they do, the flylines untangle. A good layout makes routing almost automatic. Second click adds mounting holes in the corners; doing that later usually means ripping up tracks.

7Route the tracks

  • W starts a track on a pad. Click to add corners; finish on the target pad.
  • L cycles the corner style. Shift+W cycles preset widths.
  • T and B pick the top or bottom layer. V places a via to change layers.
  • Backspace removes the last segment. Esc ends the track.
  • Red is the top copper layer and blue is the bottom, just like in the editor.
Each click routes more nets. Notice each flyline disappears once its track is done. The last click shows a layer change: the ground track goes down to a via and continues on the bottom layer in blue. On some setups routing is Alt+W instead of W; Settings, Shortcut Keys shows what yours uses.

7Add a ground pour

  • Place›Copper Pour Area. Set its net to GND, then click around the board edge.
  • The pour keeps a gap from other nets and joins every GND pad by itself.
  • Moved something? Shift+B rebuilds all pours.
  • Shift+M hides pours while you work.
A copper pour fills empty space with copper tied to ground. Watch the gaps it leaves around tracks that aren't ground. Pours go stale when you move things, which is why Shift+B matters. This is a good moment to explain why ground planes help: shorter return paths and less noise.

8Run the design rule check

  • Design›Check DRC. Errors list in the bottom panel; click one to zoom to it.
  • Common errors: copper too close together, a connection never routed, a track thinner than the rule.
  • Fix, re-run, repeat. Don't order until it reports zero errors.

DRC compares the board against the rules you set in step 6. The errors shown are examples of the usual suspects. The habit to build: run DRC, fix the first error, run again. Never send a board to a factory with DRC errors.

Check it in 3D

  • Click the 3D button in the toolbar to see the board as it will be made.
  • Look for parts that overlap and connectors that face inward.
  • Silkscreen is the printed text. Label connector pins (5V, GND), mark the LED's polarity, and add your name and a version number.
  • Silkscreen sitting on a pad won't print. Keep text off copper pads.
Editor colors become real ones: green solder mask, gold pads, white silkscreen.
The 3D view catches mistakes the 2D view hides: a connector facing the wrong way, a tall part blocking a button. Point out that the colors change: the editor uses red and blue to tell layers apart, but the real board is green solder mask with white printing.

8Export and order

  • Export›PCB Fabrication File (Gerber) makes a zip with one file per layer, plus drill files. This is what a factory needs.
  • Export›Bill of Materials (BOM) and Export›Pick and Place File if the board will be assembled.
  • Export›PDF/Image for a printable schematic.
  • Order›PCB Order sends the design straight to JLCPCB for a quote.
A circuit board is a stack of layers, and a Gerber file describes one layer. Click to pull the stack apart. Any PCB factory accepts the Gerber zip, not just JLCPCB. For this class we stop at export; ordering is optional.

Mistakes to catch before you order

  • Net labels that almost match, like VCC and VCC1, stay separate nets.
  • Right part, wrong footprint size. 0603 and 0805 are not interchangeable.
  • Editing the schematic but never running Design›Import Changes from Schematic.
  • Moving parts after pouring copper without rebuilding it with Shift+B.
  • Power tracks drawn as thin as signal tracks.
  • Connectors pointing into the board, or no mounting holes.
  • Silkscreen text printed on top of pads.
  • Ordering before DRC reports zero errors.
Every one of these has cost real students a board. Ask the class which ones the DRC would catch and which it wouldn't. DRC catches clearance and unrouted nets, but it won't tell you a connector faces the wrong way or that you picked the wrong footprint size.

Lab: build the LED button board

Your board must have

  • J1 2-pin connector, SW1 push button, R1 330 Ω resistor, D1 red LED
  • A board no larger than 40 × 30 mm, with 4 mounting holes
  • 0.25 mm signal tracks and 0.5 mm power tracks
  • A GND copper pour
  • Silkscreen with your name, "v1", J1 pin labels and LED polarity
  • Zero DRC errors

Why 330 Ω? (5 V − 2 V) ÷ 330 Ω ≈ 9 mA, a safe current for a standard LED.

Turn in

  1. Schematic PDF, from Export›PDF/Image
  2. A screenshot of the 3D view
  3. The Gerber zip, from Export›PCB Fabrication File (Gerber)
Stretch goal. Add a 555 timer so the LED blinks by itself. You'll need two resistors and a capacitor to set the rate.
