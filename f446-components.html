Capacitors, 3 digits, in pF: two digits, then that many zeros. 104 is 10 and four zeros: 100 000 pF = 100 nF. 475 is 47 and five zeros: 4.7 µF.
Resistors, 4 digits, in Ω: three digits, then zeros. 1002 is 100 and two zeros: 10 kΩ. 5101 is 5.1 kΩ.
You don't need to memorise these codes, but they let you check a part at a glance. The coloured parts are the ones that matter: size, tolerance, dielectric, voltage and value. Grey parts are the maker's series and packing codes. Each maker uses its own order, which is why the Yageo and Samsung codes look so different for the same kind of part.

Find any part in ten seconds

  • Shift+F, then paste the LCSC number from the parts list.
  • Check the result matches: same manufacturer part, same footprint, the class you expect.
  • Click Place, then click on the sheet. EasyEDA brings the symbol, the footprint and the 3D model together.
  • Searching "100nF" gives thousands of results. The C-number gives one.

100 nF at every power pin

  • The chip pulls current in short bursts on every clock edge. A capacitor right next to each VDD pin delivers them.
  • What matters is the loop: capacitor, pin, back through ground. A small loop keeps the supply clean.
  • C6 (4.7 µF) refills the small ones. It can sit anywhere on +3V3.
In the parts listC1–C5100 nF X7RC14663BasicC64.7 µF X5RC19666Basic
On the schematic the five 100 nF caps can sit in a row. On the PCB, each must be right at its own VDD and VSS pair. The drawings show why: the fast current flows around the shaded loop, and a bigger loop acts like a bigger inductor, so the supply bounces. C5 is the one on VBAT, pin 1.

C0G, X7R, X5R: why it matters which

TypeWhat it isWhere we use itWatch out
C0GCapacitance barely changes with voltage or temperature.C11–C14: the crystal load capsOnly small values, so pF-sized jobs.
X7RStays within ±15% from −55 to 125 °C.C1–C5, C8, C10: the 100 nF capsLoses capacitance as DC voltage rises.
X5RStays within ±15% from −55 to 85 °C. More µF in a small case.C6, C7, C9, C15, C16: 1 to 10 µFThe same DC-voltage loss, often more.
Rule of thumb: rate an X5R or X7R cap for at least twice the voltage it sits on. That's why the 10 µF caps on the 5 V rail are 10 V parts. Crystal caps must be C0G, or the clock drifts with temperature.
The drawing is an illustration of the trend, not real curves; each maker publishes the real DC-bias curve for each part on its product page. The point is that a 10 µF X5R cap at its full rated voltage can be well under 10 µF. Leaving a 2× margin keeps enough of it.

The analog supply and VCAP

  • FB1 is a ferrite bead: 0.45 Ω for DC, 600 Ω at 100 MHz. Power passes, digital noise doesn't.
  • C7 (1 µF) and C8 (100 nF) hold VDDA steady, because the ADC measures against it.
  • C9 on VCAP: 4.7 µF with ESR under 1 Ω. Any ceramic easily meets that.

VCAP never goes to 3.3 V. It's the output of the chip's own 1.2 V core regulator.

In the parts listFB1600 Ω beadC1002BasicC71 µFC15849BasicC8100 nFC14663BasicC94.7 µFC19666Basic
The bead is rated 200 mA, far more than VDDA draws. If you can't get a bead, a 0 Ω resistor in its place works for a first board. ESR is the capacitor's internal resistance; tantalum and electrolytic caps can be above 1 Ω, which is why we use a ceramic on VCAP.

Reset and boot

  • C10 (100 nF) on NRST filters noise so the chip doesn't reset by itself. NRST has a pull-up inside, so no resistor.
  • SW1 shorts NRST to GND: press it to restart.
  • R1 (10 kΩ) holds BOOT0 low. It's weak on purpose, so a jumper to 3.3 V can still win.

A floating BOOT0 is a classic first-board bug: the board sometimes starts ST's bootloader instead of your program and looks dead. If you ever do pull BOOT0 high to use the bootloader, PB2 (BOOT1) must be low too. The switch has four pads joined in pairs; check the symbol so it really shorts NRST to ground.

This is the whole of Sheet_2 in one picture. Three paths come out of the USB-C connector: power, data and the CC handshake. The next four slides take each part in turn.

USB-C: why two 5.1 kΩ resistors

  • A USB-C charger keeps VBUS off until it sees a 5.1 kΩ pull-down on a CC pin. Then it turns 5 V on.
  • Flip the plug and the charger talks on the other CC pin. So CC1 and CC2 each get their own resistor.
  • D+ and D− appear twice for the same reason. Join each pair.
In the parts listJ1USB-C, 16 contactsC165948ExtendedR2, R35.1 kΩC23186Basic
The charger has a pull-up on its side of the CC wire, and our 5.1 kΩ pulls it down; the voltage in between tells the charger a device is plugged in. One resistor shared by both CC pins looks like something else, and many chargers then never turn on 5 V. A USB-A to C cable will often work either way, which is how the mistake hides until someone uses a C-to-C cable.

U3: a shield for the USB pins

  • USBLC6-2SC6 clamps static zaps on D+, D− and VBUS before they reach the F446.
  • The data lines pass straight through it: D− on pins 1 and 6, D+ on pins 3 and 4.
  • Put it right next to J1, so a zap meets the clamp first.
In the parts listU3USBLC6-2SC6C7519Extended
A finger touching the connector can carry thousands of volts of static. The clamps inside U3 send that to ground and VBUS instead of into PA11 and PA12. Pins 1 and 6 are the same line inside the chip, as are 3 and 4, so the trace goes in one side and out the other.

U2: 5 V to 3.3 V

  • ME6211 is a linear regulator (LDO), good for 500 mA.
  • Pins: 1 VIN and 3 CE to VBUS, so it's always on. 2 GND, 5 VOUT to +3V3, 4 not connected.
  • C15 and C16 (10 µF ceramic) on its input and output.
  • The extra 1.7 V becomes heat. Add up what the board draws: the F446's current from its datasheet, the LEDs, anything on the headers.

D1, D2: why both LEDs are red

  • An LED needs a resistor to set its current. On 3.3 V, the LED's forward voltage Vf uses up most of the supply.
  • Red drops about 2 V, leaving 1.3 V across 1 kΩ: about 1.3 mA. Clearly lit.
  • The green we looked at drops 3.1 V, leaving 0.2 V: 0.2 mA. It looks dead.
  • So D1 (on PA5) and D2 (power) are both red, both Basic.

Y1: the 8 MHz crystal

  • 8 MHz feeds the PLL: 180 MHz for the core, and 48 MHz for USB from the second PLL.
  • Its load caps: 2 × (CL − Cstray). C0G, right at pins 5 and 6.
  • Start-up check from ST's AN2867: will the F446's oscillator start this crystal? Yes, with room to spare.

The start-up figure is g-m-crit equals 4 times ESR times 2-pi-F squared times C0 plus CL squared. With this crystal it comes to 0.43 to 0.59 milliamps per volt, depending on its shunt capacitance C0, which we assumed is 3 to 7 pF. The F446 datasheet allows up to 1. Y2: the 32.768 kHz crystal

  • Only for the real-time clock: keeping time and date, even while the chip sleeps.
  • 32 768 is 215. Halve it 15 times and you get one tick per second.
  • Pick a 6 pF crystal. Common 12.5 pF watch crystals can be too hard for this chip's low-power driver to start.
  • Ours is the Nucleo's own crystal and caps. Fit it later if you need it: mark Y2, C13, C14 as DNP (do not place).
Set LSE drive to high in firmware for extra start-up margin. It costs a few microamps.
In the parts listY232.768 kHzC1985642CheckC13, C144.3 pF C0GC59526Check
The Nucleo-F446RE uses this exact crystal, the Abracon ABS25 at 6 pF, with 4.3 pF caps, so we know it starts on this chip. If the project never needs the clock or calendar, leave these three parts off; PC14 and PC15 then become ordinary pins.

J2: program it with the Nucleo

  • Take both CN2 jumpers off. The ST-LINK lets go of the Nucleo's own chip.
  • Six wires, CN4 to J2, pin 1 to pin 1 through pin 6 to pin 6. Same order, so they run straight.
  • Power our board from its own USB-C. CN4 pin 1 only senses our 3.3 V.
  • Put the jumpers back to program the Nucleo again.
In the parts listJ21 × 6 headerC37208Extended
This is why J2's pin order copies CN4: the six jumper wires go straight across with no crossing, so there's nothing to get wrong. The drawing is simplified. If the programmer can't connect, try "Connect under reset" in CubeProgrammer, which is why NRST is on pin 5.

20 different parts, 34 pieces per board

Extended parts add a one-time loading fee per part type if JLCPCB assembles the board. Swap where it's free: the Basic 22 pF C1653 can replace the 27 pF. J2 and J3 are through-hole, so we solder those by hand.
Ten of the twenty are Basic. The chip, the USB parts and the regulator are Extended, and that's normal for parts like these. The three marked Check: confirm their class in the EasyEDA search result when you place them. If you solder the board yourself, none of this affects you.

Mistakes to look for

VCAP wired to 3.3 VC9 goes to GND and nothing else, or the chip won't run.
One resistor for both CC pinsR2 and R3, one each, or USB-C chargers stay off.
BOOT0 left floatingR1 to GND, or the board may start ST's bootloader instead of your code.
X7R on the crystalC11 to C14 must be C0G.
A green LED on 3.3 V with 1 kΩ0.2 mA looks like a dead board. Use the red one.
Only one D+ pin joinedJoin both D+ and both D−, or USB works only one way up.
J2 in a different orderMatch the Nucleo's CN4, so the wires run straight.
+3V3 here, 3V3 thereTwo names make two nets. Copy and paste the label.
Use this as the checklist for partner review. Every one of these passes the Design manager's checks, because the software can't know what you meant. Only a person reading the sheet against the parts list catches them.

Test on the Nucleo now

  • New STM32 project, board NUCLEO-F446RE.
  • Blink PA5, read the button on PC13, print "hello" on USART2 at 115200.
  • The Nucleo has no 8 MHz crystal. Its ST-LINK feeds 8 MHz into PH0, so HSE is set to bypass.
  • Our board has Y1. Change one setting and the same code runs on it.

Making and shipping boards can take one to two weeks, so the order is the deadline that matters most.

Bring-up order matters: power first with nothing else connected, measuring 3.3 V at each VDD pin and checking nothing gets hot. Only then connect the ST-LINK. If SWD connects, the chip, its power, VCAP and NRST are all right, and blinking the LED proves the clock and the firmware.
